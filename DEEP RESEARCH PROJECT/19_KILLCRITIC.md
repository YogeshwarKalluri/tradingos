# PART 19 — KILLCRITIC

*You asked me to assume you are emotionally attached and to try to prove you wrong. Here is the
hardest version of the case against.*

---

## "Why should I NOT invest in this?"

**1. You are not buying a business. You are buying a job, and paying above market for it.**

The base case's month-36 state: $1.05M of revenue, $121,194 of operating profit, 3.8 FTE, and
you working full time. Draw a market salary of $54,000 and $67,194 is left. Over three years:
$162,000 of salary plus $129,001 retained. **About $97,000 a year of total owner economics for
full-time physical work, $20,000 of at-risk capital, and a 50.9% chance the business closes.**

That is a job with a variable and unreliable salary and no benefits. **You could earn $97,000 in
Metro Detroit without risking $20,000 and without moving refrigerators.** The Detroit–Warren–
Dearborn MSA mean wage is $33.27/hour — $69,000 a year — and you are proposing to work harder
than that for a 40% premium and a coin-flip chance of losing your capital.

**2. Your central assumption — that the MSRP gap is your margin — is arithmetically wrong.**

The lot's "$53,574 MSRP" is really **$43,093** of street value; the appliances resell for
**$22,305** of asking price; you actually collect about **$20,475**. You were looking at a
number that is **2.6× the real one.** Everything downstream of that — your $8,500 bid, your
sense of the opportunity, your $1M target — inherits the error.

**3. You would be entering an auction you are structurally disadvantaged to win.**

Comparable lots clear at $188–$245 per unit. My maximum is $96–$143 landed. You lose that
auction to a buyer 100 miles from Shippensburg who pays $400 of freight instead of $1,750 — a
$29/unit advantage, 23% of your entire contribution margin, before either of you does anything
clever. **In an English auction, the winner is the bidder with the most optimistic assumptions.**
If you win at $8,500, you have not beaten anyone. You have paid $11,100 for something worth
$6,700 to you, and the market's other participants let you have it.

**4. Scale, the thing your whole plan depends on, actively destroys value here.**

| Units/mo | Op profit/mo |
|---:|---:|
| 199 | **$10,620** |
| 235 | $1,231 |
| 470 | **−$443** |

Your plan is "buy more trucks, make more money." **The model says the opposite past 199
units/month.** Every unit absorbs $91 of fixed cost regardless of price; every hire costs $57 of
labour per unit against $126.50 of contribution; every second location doubles rent and adds a
staffing floor before it adds a single sale. Growth in this business is not a flywheel — it is a
tax you pay for the privilege of being bigger.

**5. Your only pricing power sits on top of a $200 refrigerator.**

Verified Detroit-metro listings: refrigerator $200, washer $250, gas dryer $345, washer/dryer set
$625. AOK Appliance delivers free over $300. Your scratch-and-dent gas dryer at $385 appears on
the same Marketplace screen as a working used one at $345. **Detroit has the lowest used-appliance
price floor of any market in the competitor set** — that is a structural feature of the region's
income profile, not a marketing problem you can out-execute.

**6. The financing you would need does not exist.**

Inventory financing: lenders will not advance against liquidation appliance inventory. Floor plan:
franchised dealers only. PO financing: consumers do not issue POs. Vendor terms: **B-Stock is
prepaid, full stop.** SBA and lines of credit require two years of filed returns. **You are
compounding retained earnings against a 55-day cash cycle, and there is no lever to pull.**

**7. Half the simulated versions of you go out of business.**

50.9% closure rate. 31.7% chance of an outright loss over 36 months. 81.1% chance you earn less
than $4,500/month equivalent. **Median outcome: $31,482 of cumulative operating profit over three
years — about $875 a month.**

## "What assumptions are most likely wrong?"

Ranked by how much damage being wrong does:

**1. That you can buy below the market clearing price, consistently.** The entire base case rests
on $165/unit landed. The observed market is $230–$290. At $191 the business is insolvent by month
8. **This is the assumption most likely to be wrong, because it requires you to be systematically
more disciplined than every other bidder, forever, starting with zero track record.** Everything
else in this study is a footnote to this one line.

**2. That Metro Detroit will absorb 200+ units per month from a new entrant.** My ceiling of
630–840 units/month regionally is derived from my own estimates of appliance replacement rates
and secondary-market share — the weakest numbers in the study. If the real secondary share is 10%
rather than 15%, the base case's steady state falls by a third.

**3. That your realised price is 50–55% of street.** If competitors respond, or if you cannot
communicate "new, warrantied, delivered" in a Marketplace listing, you land nearer the used-market
floor. **A $25 drop in average price is $5,000/month at 200 units — roughly half of base-case
operating profit.**

**4. That 8% of a floor-loaded warehouse-damaged load is salvage.** This is estimated, not
sourced. No liquidator publishes defect rates. At 14% one truckload swings from +$2,354 to
−$5,804.

**5. That the owner sustains 1.5 FTE of unpaid labour for 12 months and 1.0 for 24 more.** Three
years of 60-hour weeks moving appliances. The model treats this as free. It is not free, and it
is not indefinitely sustainable.

**6. That 81 units per FTE per month is achievable.** It is a bottom-up standard, cross-checked
against one anecdotal press report. If the real number is 66, payroll per unit rises from $57 to
$70 — 10% of contribution — and the break-even volume shifts materially.

## "What would cause the $1M goal to fail?"

It does not fail from one thing. **It fails because $1M was never in the reachable set.**

$1,000,000 over 36 months is **$27,800 a month, every month, from a standing start.** This
business peaks at **$10,620 a month.** Even running flat-out at the peak for all 36 months —
impossible, since you start at zero — you reach $382,000.

To get there you need **both** a 12% lift in revenue per unit **and** operation at 840 units per
month, which is essentially the entire absorbable secondary-appliance demand of Metro Detroit,
four times the base case's peak, requiring three locations and roughly $500,000 of working
capital you do not have and cannot borrow.

**The specific things that kill it, in order:** paying market price for inventory (immediately
fatal); the fixed-cost step at the second location (kills it at ~235 units/month); the demand
ceiling of one storefront (~220 units/month); the absence of inventory financing (caps the
growth rate at retained earnings); and the 55-day cash cycle (means every dollar of growth is
locked up for two months before it returns).

## "What evidence would invalidate the business model?"

Concrete, falsifiable tests. **Run them before you spend $11,000, not after.**

1. **Landed cost.** If your first three loads land above **$180/unit**, the model is invalid.
   Stop.
2. **Realised price.** Sell 15 units. If your average realised merchandise price is below
   **$320**, the model is invalid. (Base case: $358.)
3. **30-day sell-through.** If under **38%** of a load sells in 30 days, the model is invalid.
   (Base: 45%.)
4. **Salvage rate.** If more than **12%** of a load is salvage-only, that source is invalid.
5. **Labour.** Time yourself. If you exceed **2.1 hours per unit** all-in, the payroll model is
   invalid. (Standard: 1.65.)
6. **Auctions won.** If you win **more than 20%** of the auctions you bid your calculated maximum
   on, your maximum is too high — recheck your comps.
7. **Ancillary attach.** If delivery attach is under **30%** or protection-plan attach under
   **8%**, $20/unit of contribution disappears.

**Test 1 and test 3 are the ones that matter. If either fails, close the position.**

## "What is the strongest argument that this CAN become a $1M-profit business?"

Made honestly, because it is not nothing:

**1. The unit economics at small scale are genuinely excellent.** 60.7% gross margin, 30%
contribution margin, and **18.9% operating margin at one truck a month** with zero employees.
Break-even is **12 units per month**. Very few businesses available for $20,000 have those
characteristics. The reason my answer is "no" is about scaling, not about whether the core
transaction works — **it works.**

**2. There is a real, durable, geographic edge available and nobody is exploiting it.**
**Whirlpool is headquartered in Benton Harbor, Michigan**, with plants across Ohio; GE is in
Louisville. A Detroit operator sourcing 150–250-mile loads pays $13–$21/unit of freight against
$31–$45 for a Pennsylvania load. **That $20–$25 per unit is 16–20% of the entire contribution
margin, it is structural, and it cannot be competed away by anyone outside Michigan and Ohio.**
If you built the model around that instead of around B-Stock auctions, the landed-cost
assumption — assumption #1, the one most likely to be wrong — becomes far more defensible.
**This is the single strongest argument in the whole study and it deserves to be taken
seriously.**

**3. The path from 199 units to 840 exists — it just is not a 36-month path.** Appliance Outlet
Texas runs 8 locations in one metro. Robert Stevens has 65 years. Scratch-N-Dent Superstore has
36. **Multi-location works when the second site is a showroom on shared infrastructure rather
than a second business** — my model charges a full staffing floor and separate rent per site,
which is the correct assumption for a new operator and the wrong one for a mature one. A
disciplined operator who reaches 200 units/month by year three, then adds satellite showrooms in
years four through eight on one warehouse, one fleet and one buying desk, **could** plausibly
reach $300–$500K of annual operating profit by year eight. **$1M cumulative would then arrive
around year six or seven.**

**4. The B2B channel improves return on capital.** $3.25–$3.67 of contribution per unit per day
against $2.29 for retail, and it converts your worst inventory from a write-off into cash. It
does not fix absolute profit, but it makes the capital work harder — which matters more than
margin when you are capital-constrained.

**5. And you would own something.** At $121,194 of operating profit the business is worth
roughly $250K–$400K on the comparable multiples in Part 21. That is real equity that a salaried
job does not build.

**The strongest honest case is therefore: this is a good $250K-over-three-years business, with a
real edge available in short-haul Michigan/Ohio sourcing, and a credible path to $1M cumulative
profit by roughly year six or seven — not year three.**

**Change the timeline and the answer changes. Keep the 36-month target and the answer is no.**
