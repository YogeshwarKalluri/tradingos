## D. The five that look genuinely successful, and what they do differently

**Robert Stevens (Philadelphia), Appliance Outlet Texas (8 stores), Scratch-N-Dent Superstore
(San Antonio, 36 years), St. Louis Appliance Outlet, and Discount Appliance's owner-operator
network.**

Five patterns separate them from the failures, and **four of the five run directly against
your plan:**

**1. They are decades old, and they compounded rather than sprinted.** Scratch-N-Dent
Superstore: founded 1990. Robert Stevens: 65+ years. Appliance Outlet Texas reached 8 stores
over many years, not 36 months. **Nobody in this industry got large fast.** The compounding
is slow because contribution per unit is thin and inventory ties up cash.

**2. They own supply relationships, not auction accounts.** "Tractor-trailers arriving
weekly" (St. Louis) is a *programme* with a manufacturer or regional distributor, not a
sequence of won auctions. A programme means a negotiated price, predictable condition, and
short freight. **Auction buying is what you do before you have a programme, and it is the
lowest-margin way to source.** This is the single biggest gap between you and them.

**3. They dominate one metro before adding a second.** Appliance Outlet Texas is 8 stores in
*one* metro, sharing one warehouse, one delivery fleet, one marketing spend, one buying desk.
That is how you beat the fixed-cost step my model shows at the second location — the second
site is a *showroom* on shared infrastructure, not a second business. **If you scale, scale
this way.**

**4. The profitable small ones stay small on purpose.** Discount Appliance's explicit pitch is
under 3,000 sq ft, single owner-operator, **no employees**, "keeping operating costs low while
the operator keeps all the profit." That is not modesty, it is the maths: at $126.50 of
contribution per unit and $4,600 per fully-loaded FTE, the first employee costs you 36 units a
month before contributing anything. **The industry's most reliably profitable configuration is
the one that never hires.**

**5. They are geographically insulated.** San Antonio, Cedar Rapids, Charlotte, Lancaster PA.
Metro Detroit is not that. It has 25–35 direct competitors, ABC Warehouse as a well-capitalised
chain incumbent with used and S&D lines, and — most importantly — the lowest used-appliance
price floor of any of these markets. **Detroit is the hardest of these markets, not the
easiest.**

## E. What this means for you

The competitive set tells you the winning configuration is: **one metro, owner-operated,
sub-3,000 sq ft to start, sourced from a negotiated short-haul programme, grown over a decade.**

That configuration is a good business. It is also, precisely, **not** a $1M-in-36-months
business, and the operators who have actually succeeded here are evidence against the plan
rather than for it.
