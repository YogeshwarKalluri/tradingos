## Why the concept looks better than it is

The whole thesis rests on the gap between manifest MSRP and acquisition cost. That gap is
mostly an illusion, and it closes in three steps:

| Step | Value | % of manifest MSRP | Basis |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Manifest "extended retail" | $53,574 | 100% | The auction listing |
| **Actual street price of the same units, new** | **$43,093** | **80.4%** | Verified live prices, 13 model classes |
| Realistic scratch-and-dent asking prices, Metro Detroit | $22,305 | 41.6% | 50–55% of street |
| **Revenue you actually collect** (condition mix, negotiation, dead stock, plus delivery/install/warranty) | **$20,475** | **38.2%** | Model, base case |

So the honest revenue on a "$53,574 MSRP" truckload is about **$20,500**, not $53,574 and not
even the $26,800 that "50% of MSRP" would suggest. Three separate haircuts stack:

1. **MSRP is inflated.** The GE Profile PFW870SPVRS lists at $1,449–$1,739 and sells at
   $949–$999. The Samsung WA45T3200AW has an $849 MSRP and a $599–$614 street price.
2. **Scratch-and-dent sells at ~50–55% of street**, not 50–70% off MSRP, because Metro
   Detroit's used-appliance floor is low — Craigslist has LG gas dryers at $345 and
   refrigerators at $200.
3. **Not every unit sells.** Base case: 8% salvage-only, plus negotiation and end-of-life
   markdowns.

## Why scale does not rescue it

Contribution is **$126.50 per unit** on **$420** of revenue — 30% contribution margin. Labour
costs **$4,600 per fully-loaded FTE** per month against a bottom-up standard of **81 units per
FTE**, which is **$57/unit of labour** the moment you stop doing the work yourself. Add rent
and the fixed cost per unit reaches roughly $85–$95. There is very little left.

The consequence is that **profit is not monotonic in volume**:

| Trucks/mo | Units/mo | Revenue/mo | Op profit/mo | Margin | Paid FTE | Sites |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 47 | $19,740 | **$3,729** | 18.9% | 0 (owner) | 1 |
| 2 | 94 | $39,480 | **$5,705** | 14.4% | 0.2 | 1 |
| 3 | 141 | $59,220 | **$7,571** | 12.8% | 0.7 | 1 |
| **4.2** | **199** | **$83,580** | **$10,620 ← peak** | **12.7%** | **1.5** | **1** |
| 5 | 235 | $98,700 | $1,231 | 1.2% | 4.4 | 2 |
| 10 | 470 | $197,400 | **−$443** | −0.2% | 9.8 | 3 |
| 20 | 940 | $394,800 | $842 | 0.2% | 18.6 | 5 |

**The business peaks at about 199 units per month and roughly $1.0M of annual revenue, and
then growth destroys profit.** Every step past a single location adds rent and a staffing
floor faster than it adds contribution. This is the single most important finding in the
study, and it is fatal to a $1M-in-36-months plan.

## What would have to be true for $1M

Volume alone cannot do it. Holding the base cost structure and raising only revenue per unit:

| Revenue/unit | Best 36-mo cumulative profit | Clears $1M? |
|---:|---:|:--:|
| $420 (base) | $256,197 | no |
| $445 (+6%) | $662,256 | no |
| **$470 (+12%)** | **$1,166,441** | **yes** |
| $520 (+24%) | $2,174,813 | yes |

So $1M requires **about a 12% lift in revenue per unit sustained for three years, while
simultaneously running at the market's absorption ceiling of ~840 units/month.** That means
premium-skewed inventory, disciplined pricing with no discounting, a real attach rate on
delivery/install/warranty, and near-total regional dominance — all at once, starting from
$20,000. The optimistic scenario in Part 15 is what that conjunction looks like; it requires
roughly ten favourable conditions to hold simultaneously, which is why it never occurs in a
random draw.

## Final question

> *If you personally had $20,000 and wanted a business capable of $1,000,000+ cumulative
> profit within 36 months, would you choose this appliance liquidation model?*

# NO.

Three reasons, in order of weight.

**1. The arithmetic does not reach the goal, at any volume.** $1M of cumulative profit over
36 months is an average of $27,800 per month for three straight years, starting from zero.
This business peaks at roughly $10,600 per month before it starts destroying value on the way
up. To clear $1M you would need to run at nearly the entire absorbable secondary-appliance
demand of Metro Detroit, at a 12% better price than the market pays, from month one. There is
no month-36 volume that fixes a $126.50 contribution margin.

**2. The margin of safety is negative.** The gap between "works" ($165/unit landed) and "loses
everything" ($191/unit landed) is 16% of one input — an input set by open auction against
bidders who have shorter freight, no showroom, and value their own labour at zero. You would
be competing on price for inputs against people whose cost structure is better than yours,
in a market where the loser wins the auction. Half of simulated paths end in closure.

**3. The thing you would actually be buying is a job.** In the base case you finish month 36
running a $1.05M-revenue business, working full time, having drawn $162,000 in salary over
three years and accumulated $129,000 of retained profit. That is a real outcome and not a
contemptible one — it is roughly $97,000/year of total owner economics. But it is a
self-employment outcome, and it is available with far less capital risk and far less physical
labour in several of the alternatives scored in Part 20.

**What I would do instead, if committed to this industry:** run it deliberately as a
one-location, ~200-unit/month, ~$1M-revenue owner-operated business — the peak of the profit
curve — sourcing short-haul Whirlpool/Ohio loads rather than 500-mile B-Stock lots, with no
showroom for the first year. Target $120K–$150K of annual operating profit by year three and
never open the second location. That plan is achievable, is roughly a 30% probability
outcome, and does not require pretending the $1M number is reachable.
